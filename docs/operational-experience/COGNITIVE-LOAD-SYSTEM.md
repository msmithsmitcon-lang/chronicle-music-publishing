# Cognitive Load System

The Cognitive Load System defines how Plexicon-governed systems prevent users from becoming confused, overloaded, or afraid to act.

Operational systems must expose enough truth to support action while avoiding unnecessary cognitive chaos.

## Information Density

Information density should match the task and risk level.

Dense screens are appropriate when users need to compare, monitor, triage, or operate repeatedly. Sparse screens are appropriate when users need orientation, learning, or a single clear action.

Density must be organized through:

- hierarchy
- grouping
- labels
- alignment
- progressive detail
- consistent status language

## Progressive Disclosure

Systems should reveal detail as it becomes useful.

Progressive disclosure may include:

- summary first, detail on demand
- collapsed diagnostics
- expandable audit trails
- staged forms
- contextual advanced controls
- role-based visibility

Disclosure must not hide critical risk, blocked states, or required approvals.

## Decision Complexity

Decision complexity should be reduced through structure, not by concealing truth.

Interfaces should clarify:

- what decision is being made
- why it matters
- what information supports it
- what options exist
- what happens next
- whether the decision can be reversed

## Task Chunking

Complex work should be broken into coherent chunks.

Good chunks have:

- a clear purpose
- visible completion criteria
- limited decision load
- understandable dependencies
- recoverable stopping points

## AI Assistance Boundaries

AI should reduce cognitive burden without taking unclear authority.

AI assistance must make clear:

- whether it is suggesting, summarizing, validating, or executing
- whether confidence is high or low
- whether human approval is required
- what source or state it used
- what remains uncertain

## System-Driven and User-Driven Decisions

Some decisions should be system-driven, such as deterministic validation or eligibility checks.

Some decisions must remain user-driven, especially where judgment, accountability, approval, or risk acceptance is involved.

The interface must distinguish between these decision types.

## Avoiding Operational Overwhelm

Operational overwhelm happens when users see too many unresolved states, unclear warnings, or competing actions at once.

To avoid it:

- prioritize next actions
- group alerts by severity
- avoid duplicate warnings
- use plain-language status
- expose dependencies clearly
- separate urgent from informational states

## Plain-Language Status Messaging

Status messages should be direct and operationally meaningful.

Prefer messages that explain:

- what is happening
- what is needed
- who owns the next step
- whether action is safe
- what happens if no action is taken

Avoid vague messages that make the user guess what the system knows.
